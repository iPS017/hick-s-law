# Ethics check: choice architecture vs dark patterns

Reducing choices to help a visitor decide is choice architecture. Reducing choices or hiding information so the visitor decides something they would not decide with full information is a dark pattern. It also costs money later (refunds, chargebacks, churn, complaints) and carries legal risk.

The test for every proposed change: **would the visitor still agree with the decision if they saw exactly what we did?** If not, drop the change.

## Allowed

- Recommending one plan that genuinely fits most visitors, with a truthful label.
- Preselecting a free, reversible option that most visitors would pick (default shipping method, billing address same as shipping, most common seat count).
- Moving rarely needed options behind one visible disclosure control.
- Removing plans, fields or options that almost nobody needs.
- Ordering options so the most relevant come first.
- Asking a question in onboarding to personalise the path.

## Not allowed

- Preselected checkboxes that add cost (insurance, add-ons, donations) or consent (marketing emails).
- Hiding a cheaper plan that still exists and is sold, or making it impossible to find.
- A "Most popular" or "Recommended" badge that is not true.
- A plan that exists only as a decoy and that nobody can sensibly buy.
- Showing a monthly equivalent while hiding the total billed, or revealing fees at the last step (drip pricing).
- Fake urgency or scarcity (countdowns that reset, "only 2 left" when false).
- Guilt-trip decline links ("No, I don't want to grow my business").
- Making cancellation or downgrade much harder than signup.
- Removing the visible way to decline an upsell.

## Legal pointers (not legal advice)

- EU Consumer Rights Directive, Article 22: an extra payment beyond the main obligation needs the consumer's express consent; preselected boxes do not count.
- EU Unfair Commercial Practices Directive: misleading and aggressive practices, including in interface design, can be challenged. The European Commission's guidance covers dark patterns under it.
- EU Digital Services Act, Article 25: online platforms must not design interfaces that deceive or manipulate users or impair their ability to make free and informed decisions.
- GDPR: consent for marketing must be freely given and opt-in.
- The EU has announced a Digital Fairness Act targeting dark patterns, drip pricing and hard-to-cancel subscriptions. Check its current status before relying on it.
- Outside the EU, consumer protection authorities (for example the US FTC and the UK CMA) also act against deceptive design. Rules change; tell the user to check with counsel for their market.

When a requested change falls in "Not allowed", say so plainly, explain the risk in one or two sentences, and propose the honest alternative that gets most of the benefit.
