# Evidence grades

Check here before quoting a number. If a claim is not listed, treat it as grade D until a primary source is found.

## Contents
- Grades
- Grade A: peer-reviewed or meta-analytic
- Grade B: rigorous industry research or large benchmarks
- Grade C: single cases and practitioner datasets
- Grade D: do not quote
- Sources

## Grades

- **A**: peer-reviewed study or meta-analysis. Cite with author and year.
- **B**: large benchmark or rigorous industry research, method described. Cite with source, mention it is observational if it is.
- **C**: one company's test, a practitioner dataset, a case study. Present as "one case" or "one dataset".
- **D**: number with no traceable study, method or sample. Do not quote.

## Grade A: peer-reviewed or meta-analytic

| Claim | Source |
|---|---|
| Choice reaction time grows with log2(n + 1) of equally likely alternatives | Hick 1952; Hyman 1953 |
| Hick's law does not support "less is always better" in HCI; visual search and decision processes matter more | Liu et al., CHI 2020 |
| Mean choice-overload effect near zero across 63 conditions (N = 5,036), high variance | Scheibehenne, Greifeneder, Todd 2010, J. Consumer Research |
| Choice overload is significant when set complexity, task difficulty, preference uncertainty and an effort-minimising goal are present (99 observations, N = 7,202) | Chernev, Bockenholt, Goodman 2015, J. Consumer Psychology |
| Defaults strongly shift choices (organ donation; online test about 82% opt-out vs 42% opt-in) | Johnson, Goldstein 2003, Science |
| Adding an asymmetrically dominated option raises the target's share | Huber, Payne, Puto 1982, J. Consumer Research |

## Grade B: rigorous industry research or large benchmarks

| Claim | Source |
|---|---|
| Average US checkout shows 23.48 form elements; an ideal flow can use about 12 | Baymard Institute, checkout usability research |
| Checkout design improvements could lift conversion about 35% for the average large e-commerce site | Baymard Institute |
| Average documented cart abandonment around 70% (average of about 50 studies) | Baymard Institute |
| Progressive disclosure improves learnability, efficiency and error rate | Nielsen Norman Group |
| Landing pages with one link or CTA averaged 13.5% conversion vs 11.9% (two) and 10.5% (three), 18,639 pages, observational | Unbounce benchmark report |

## Grade C: single cases and practitioner datasets

| Claim | Source | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Jam booth: about 30% of stoppers bought with 6 jams vs about 3% with 24 | Iyengar, Lepper 2000 | Peer-reviewed, but one store, two Saturdays, about 35 purchases, mixed replications |
| Number of tiers barely changes conversion while ARPU rises with tiers | Evan Grubb, The Tiering Dilemma | Practitioner analysis |
| Teams test both adding and cutting tiers; 3 is a midpoint | Lazyweb Research, 2026 | Automated detection of experiments, method partly opaque |
| A second CTA for a distinct intent raised overall leads 17% | Tinuiti case study (Culligan) | One client |
| Economist subscription decoy | Dan Ariely, Predictably Irrational | Classroom test of about 100 students, not a field test |

## Grade D: do not quote

These circulate widely without a traceable primary study. If the user cites one, say it is not traceable and propose to measure instead.

- "A single CTA gets 371% more clicks" (and variants: 266%, 1,617% sales)
- "Three tiers convert 40% better than five" or "30% higher ARPU across 512 companies"
- "More than four tiers cuts conversion by 13% / 17% / 20 to 30%"
- "Each extra onboarding question reduces completion by 10 to 15%"
- "Red buttons convert 21% better"
- Any "X% lift" quoted by a content farm without a link to the study
